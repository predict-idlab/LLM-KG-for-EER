To maximize energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness for this 1862 detached building, the renovation should follow the **"Fabric First"** principle: reduce energy demand through insulation and airtightness before upgrading heating and energy generation systems.

### **Building Analysis Summary**
*   **Strengths:** The roof is already well-insulated; window frames are in good condition.
*   **Weaknesses:** Single-glazing windows; uninsulated cavity walls; uninsulated floor above crawl space; inefficient heating/emission system.
*   **Constraints:** No potential for geothermal energy; no access to a district heat grid.
*   **Goal:** Improve the EPC label (currently 165/B).

---

### **Proposed Renovation Path**

#### **Phase 1: Thermal Envelope (Demand Reduction)**
*Focus: Stopping heat loss using the most cost-effective methods.*

1.  **Insulate Wall Cavity:** All four external walls are cavity walls and currently not insulated. This is the highest priority as it offers a massive reduction in heat loss with low nuisance and low cost per $m^2$.
2.  **Replace Glazing:** Since the wooden window frames are in "Good State," it is not cost-effective to replace the entire window. Instead, replace the **Single Glazing** with high-efficiency double or triple glazing.
3.  **Insulate Floor Above Basement:** Insulating the floor above the crawl space prevents cold air from entering the living areas from below.

#### **Phase 2: Ventilation (Air Quality & Efficiency)**
*Focus: Ensuring healthy air exchange in a now better-insulated home.*

4.  **Replace Ventilation System:** With the envelope tightened (walls, windows, floor), the existing Ventilation System A (natural) should be upgraded to a more controlled system (e.g., System C or D) to prevent condensation and maintain thermal comfort.

#### **Phase 3: Heating & Energy Generation (Operational Performance)**
*Focus: Switching to sustainable energy sources to power the efficient envelope.*

5.  **Place Air-Water Heat Pump:** Since the building has a reason to change its emission system and cannot use geothermal or heat grids, an air-water heat pump is the most efficient choice. It integrates well with existing radiators and floor heating.
6.  **Place PV-Panels on Roof:** To offset the electricity consumption of the new heat pump and lower long-term operational costs, install solar panels on the roof (the building has confirmed potential for PV).

---

### **Optimal Sequence & Logic**

| Step | Action | Logic | Cost-Effectiveness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Wall & Floor Insulation** | Reduces the total heating load. | High (Low cost/High impact) |
| **2** | **Glazing Upgrade** | Eliminates major thermal bridges. | High (Saves frame costs) |
| **3** | **Ventilation Upgrade** | Prevents moisture in airtight home. | Medium |
| **4** | **Air-Water Heat Pump** | Sized correctly for the *reduced* load. | Medium (High cap-ex, low op-ex) |
| **5** | **PV-Panels** | Powers the heat pump with green energy. | High (Long-term ROI) |

### **Final Expert Recommendation**
By insulating the cavity walls and upgrading the glazing first, you significantly lower the building's energy demand. This allows you to install a **smaller, less expensive heat pump** than would be required for the building in its current state, maximizing both the initial budget and the long-term energy performance (EPC).
