To maximize energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness for this 1951 semi-detached building, I propose a **"Fabric First"** renovation strategy. By first reducing the energy demand of the building shell, we can install a smaller, more efficient heating system, thereby reducing both capital expenditure and long-term operational costs.

### **Phase 1: Thermal Envelope (The "Fabric First" Approach)**
The building currently has an EPC score of 388 (D label), with several critical areas lacking insulation. Addressing these first is the most cost-effective way to lower the energy score.

1.  **Insulate Gable Roof:** The main roof is currently not insulated. This is the highest priority as heat rises; insulating the gable roof will provide the most immediate impact on thermal comfort and energy loss.
2.  **Insulate Wall Exterior:** The front, rear, and right walls are full brick and not insulated. While interior insulation is an option, exterior insulation is significantly more effective for energy efficiency and prevents internal condensation. (Note: Since they are "Full Brick," cavity insulation is likely not applicable).
3.  **Insulate Floor Above Basement:** The floor above the basement is not insulated. This is a relatively low-nuisance intervention that prevents "cold feet" and reduces heat loss to the basement.
4.  **Insulate Flat Roof:** The roof extension is only moderately insulated. Upgrading this to a high insulation grade will complete the thermal seal of the building.

### **Phase 2: Ventilation and Air Quality**
Once the building is insulated and "sealed," air exchange is reduced, which can lead to moisture issues if not managed.

5.  **Replace Ventilation System:** Upgrade from Ventilation System A (natural supply) to a more efficient system (such as System D). This ensures a healthy indoor climate and can incorporate heat recovery to prevent the loss of the heat we just "trapped" with the new insulation.

### **Phase 3: Energy Generation & Emission Systems**
With the energy demand now significantly lowered, we can replace the outdated boiler with a sustainable alternative.

6.  **Place Air-Water Heat Pump:** The building has a reason to change its emission system, but lacks the site potential for geothermal drilling and is not connected to a heat grid. An air-water heat pump is the most logical choice as it integrates directly with the existing radiators and floor heating.
7.  **Place Emission System on Low Temperature:** To maximize the COP (Coefficient of Performance) of the new heat pump, the existing radiators should be evaluated and potentially upgraded to low-temperature emitters.

### **Phase 4: Renewable Energy Integration**
The final step is to offset the electricity consumption of the new heat pump.

8.  **Place PV-Panels on Roof:** The building has confirmed potential for PV. Installing solar panels will provide the electricity needed to run the air-water heat pump, drastically reducing the building's operational carbon footprint and yearly energy costs.

---

### **Summary of the Renovation Path**

| Sequence | Action | Target Element | Primary Benefit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Insulate Gable Roof** | RoofMain | Stop major heat loss (Highest ROI) |
| **2** | **Insulate Wall Exterior** | Walls (Front, Rear, Right) | Massive reduction in heat transfer |
| **3** | **Insulate Floor** | FloorAboveBasement | Improve ground-level thermal comfort |
| **4** | **Replace Ventilation** | VentilationSystem | Maintain air quality in a sealed house |
| **5** | **Air-Water Heat Pump** | Generation_System | Shift from fossil fuels to electricity |
| **6** | **PV-Panels on Roof** | RoofMain | Power the heat pump with free energy |

**Conclusion:** This sequence ensures that the owner does not "over-invest" in a heating system that is too large for a well-insulated house, and it directly addresses the motivation **"To Improve EPC"** by tackling the most deficient elements first.
