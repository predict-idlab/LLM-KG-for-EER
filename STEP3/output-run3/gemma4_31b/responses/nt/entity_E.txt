Based on the provided building data and the RENO ontology, the objective is to improve the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) score for a detached building from 1862. The building currently has a relatively good EPC score (165), but significant energy leaks exist in the walls and windows.

To maximize energy efficiency while remaining cost-effective, I propose a **"Fabric First"** approach. This ensures that we reduce the building's heat demand before investing in expensive heating technology, allowing for a smaller, more efficient heat pump.

### Proposed Renovation Path

#### Phase 1: Thermal Envelope Optimization (High ROI)
The priority is to stop heat loss through the most vulnerable elements.
1.  **Insulate Wall Cavities:** All four exterior walls are cavity walls and are currently not insulated. Cavity wall insulation is the most cost-effective method to significantly reduce heat loss without altering the building's facade.
2.  **Replace Window Glazing:** The bedrooms and living area currently use **Single Glazing**, which is highly inefficient. Since the existing wooden frames are in "Good State" (value 8), I recommend **replacing the glazing** (upgrading to double or triple glazing) rather than replacing the entire window unit. This saves on material costs while achieving similar thermal gains.
3.  **Insulate Floor Above Basement:** The building has a floor above a crawl space. Insulating this area will eliminate cold drafts from below and improve overall thermal comfort.

#### Phase 2: Ventilation Upgrade
4.  **Replace Ventilation System:** The building currently uses a Type A system (natural supply, mechanical exhaust). Upgrading to a more modern system (such as Type B or D) will reduce the amount of conditioned air lost to the outside and improve indoor air quality.

#### Phase 3: Heating System Transition
Now that the heat demand is minimized, we address the generation and emission systems.
5.  **Install an Air-Water Heat Pump:** The building owner has expressed a reason to change the emission system, and geothermal options are not viable for this site. An air-water heat pump is the most sustainable replacement for the existing boiler, especially since the building already possesses some floor heating and radiators.
6.  **Transition to Low-Temperature Emission:** To maximize the efficiency (COP) of the new heat pump, the existing radiators should be evaluated and, where necessary, replaced with **low-temperature emission systems**.

#### Phase 4: Renewable Energy Integration
7.  **Place PV Panels on Roof:** The building has high potential for solar energy. Installing PV panels will offset the electricity consumption of the new heat pump and ventilation system, moving the building toward net-zero operational energy.

### Summary of Sequence and Logic

| Sequence | Action | Logic | Cost-Effectiveness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Wall Cavity Insulation** | Lowest cost per kWh saved. | Very High |
| **2** | **Glazing Upgrade** | Eliminates major thermal bridges. | High (reusing frames) |
| **3** | **Floor Insulation** | Stops rising cold/damp. | Medium |
| **4** | **Ventilation Upgrade** | Reduces heat loss via air exchange. | Medium |
| **5** | **Air-Water Heat Pump** | Switches to sustainable generation. | Medium (Long-term ROI) |
| **6** | **PV Panels** | Powers the new efficient systems. | High (Energy offset) |

**Final Result:** This path transforms an 1862 structure into a modern, energy-efficient home by systematically reducing demand before upgrading supply, ensuring the lowest possible operational costs and the highest possible EPC improvement.
