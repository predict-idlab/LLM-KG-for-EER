To maximize energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness for this 19th-century detached building, I propose a **"Fabric First"** renovation path. By optimizing the building envelope before upgrading technical systems, we reduce the total energy demand, allowing for a smaller, more efficient, and less expensive heating system.

### Phase 1: The Building Envelope (Reducing Demand)
The priority is to stop heat loss. The building has several critical weaknesses in its shell.

1.  **Cavity Wall Insulation (`insulate wall cavity`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** All four walls are cavity walls and currently "Not Insulated." 
    *   **Justification:** This is the most cost-effective insulation method available. Since there is no historical protection, injecting insulation into existing cavities provides a massive reduction in heat loss with low nuisance and low cost.
2.  **Glazing Upgrade (`replace glazing`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** Both bedrooms and living areas have "Single Glazing," which is thermally inefficient. However, the wooden frames are in "Good State" (Value: 8).
    *   **Justification:** Instead of replacing the entire window unit, replacing only the glazing is significantly more cost-effective while achieving similar thermal gains.
3.  **Floor Insulation (`insulate floor above basement`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** The building has a floor above a crawl space/basement.
    *   **Justification:** This prevents cold drafts from the ground and improves the overall thermal comfort of the living spaces.

### Phase 2: Air Quality and Ventilation (System Balance)
Once the building is better insulated and the glazing is upgraded, the house becomes more airtight.

4.  **Ventilation Upgrade (`replace ventilation system`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** The building currently uses Ventilation System A (natural supply/exhaust).
    *   **Justification:** To prevent condensation and maintain indoor air quality after the shell is tightened in Phase 1, upgrading to a more controlled system (such as System C or D) is essential.

### Phase 3: Energy Generation and Heating (Operational Performance)
With a reduced energy load, we can now transition from inefficient electric heating to a sustainable system.

5.  **Heating System Transition (`place air-water heatpump`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** The building has a stated reason to change its emission system. Geothermal options are unavailable (no potential for horizontal or vertical drilling), and there is no heat grid.
    *   **Justification:** An air-water heat pump is the most efficient alternative. Because we insulated the walls and windows first, the heat pump can be sized smaller, reducing both installation and operational costs.
6.  **Renewable Energy Integration (`place PV-panels on roof`):** 
    *   **Analysis:** The building has confirmed potential for PV.
    *   **Justification:** To offset the electricity consumption of the new heat pump and improve the EPC label, installing solar panels is the final step toward energy independence.

---

### Summary of the Optimal Sequence

| Sequence | Action | Impact | Cost-Effectiveness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Insulate Wall Cavity** | High $\downarrow$ Heat Loss | Very High (Low cost/sqm) |
| **2** | **Replace Glazing** | High $\downarrow$ Heat Loss | High (Retains good frames) |
| **3** | **Insulate Floor** | Medium $\downarrow$ Heat Loss | Medium |
| **4** | **Replace Ventilation** | High $\uparrow$ Air Quality | Medium |
| **5** | **Air-Water Heat Pump** | High $\downarrow$ Energy Cost | Medium (Optimized by Phase 1) |
| **6** | **PV-Panels on Roof** | High $\downarrow$ Carbon/Cost | High (Long-term ROI) |

**Final Outcome:** This path directly addresses the owner's motivation to **Improve the EPC label** by systematically moving the building from a "B" label toward an "A" or "A+" label through a logical, engineering-backed progression.
